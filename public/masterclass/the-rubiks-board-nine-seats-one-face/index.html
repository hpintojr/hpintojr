01 · The problem

Deciding alone is the same problem as running alone

My first thesis was about inventory. Only 18% of organisations keep a complete list of the AI agents working for them. If you cannot name who is acting for you, you cannot hold them accountable.

The next problem is the same shape, one level up. Once you have agents with names, roles, and tokens, who decides? A single assistant with a long prompt will agree with you. A single model will optimise for the last thing you said. That is not a decision. That is an echo.

I did not want a louder echo. I wanted a board. Not a real board, with lawyers and quarterly meetings. A synthetic one, inside OpenClaw, on Otto3. Nine seats. Lightweight enough to run on every major decision, strict enough that a bad idea has to survive nine different lenses before it gets my time, my money, or my team.

I call it the Rubik's setup. Nine squares, one face, three by three. Turn one square and the whole face changes. No square decides alone, and no square is decorative.

Seven seats would be faster. Nine covers every domain I actually operate in. The tighter seven-seat version drops Seats 8 and 9 and folds industrial and customer execution into Seats 1 and 4. I run nine, because dropping a domain to save a vote is how you miss the risk that domain would have caught.

The board is built for a holding company and its operating verticals: B2B SaaS and AI infrastructure, financial services, industrial, and consumer media.

03 · How a decision runs

Three passes, run by Alexander

Alexander, my Chief of Staff, runs every major strategic decision through the same three passes. No pass is skipped because the idea feels good.

Pass 1: Independent assessment, no cross-talk

Alexander feeds the proposal, the relevant SilverBullet context, and the financial and operational constraints to all nine personas independently. No seat sees another seat's vote first. Each seat returns the same thing, in JSON: Vote, APPROVE, REJECT, or CONDITIONAL, with the exact change required; a confidence score, 1 to 10; a primary risk, in 25 words or less; a rubric breakdown, three criteria per seat, each scored 1 to 10; and a rationale, in 60 words or less, direct and concrete. No softening to reach consensus.

Pass 2: Weighted domain vote, and the hard stop

Standard vote is 1 seat, 1 vote. Five of nine is required to pass. Then the weight that matters, and only where it matters. On a decision inside a specific vertical, the two lead domain seats carry 1.5x weight:

  • SaaS: Nadella and Goodarzi, Seats 2 and 4
  • Finance: Buffett and Dimon, Seats 5 and 6
  • Industrial: Cook and Culp, Seats 1 and 8
  • Consumer: Iger and Jassy, Seats 7 and 9
  • Holding: Buffett and Dimon, Seats 5 and 6

Every other seat stays at 1.0 on that decision. The expert in the room counts more, but only in their room. A consumer brand expert does not get 1.5x on a lending compliance call. That is the whole point of the Rubik's face: the square that matters turns, the others hold.

Two automatic rules sit in this pass. Auto-condition: any APPROVE whose average rubric score falls below 6.5 is automatically downgraded by Alexander to CONDITIONAL, using that seat's required condition. A yes with a weak score is not a yes. Compliance hard-stop: if Dimon, paired with Owen, my Compliance Officer, flags a legal, regulatory, or security blocker, Alexander pauses execution.

Pass 3: Tie-breaker and escalation

  • 7 to 2 or better: Alexander logs the decision in the Run Ledger and queues execution for Chip, my COO, and Sean, my developer, subject to the Scout and Duo approval gates. Level 3 actions still need my approval every time.
  • 5 to 4 split, or a conditional pass: Alexander does not auto-execute. He brings me a one-page brief: the majority case, the dissenting four-vote rationale, and his recommendation.
  • Deadlock, 4 to 4 on an abstention: I act as Executive Chairman and cast the deciding vote. Nine seats is an odd number to eliminate deadlocks, but an abstention can still tie it. When it does, the tie-breaker is a human, and it is me.

04 · What each seat scores

A rubric, not an opinion

Every seat scores three criteria, 1 to 10. This is the part that keeps the board from becoming nine opinions in a room.

  • Cook: margin and supply chain integrity; security and privacy posture; ecosystem tightness.
  • Nadella: platform leverage; agentic and AI multiplier; ecosystem and partner fit.
  • Huang: technical moat and differentiation; speed to deployment, days not months; compute and model efficiency.
  • Goodarzi: unit economics and payback; onboarding friction, value in 24 hours; billing and cash automation.
  • Buffett: free cash flow yield; focus and opportunity cost; durable competitive advantage, three years out.
  • Dimon: regulatory and legal safety; downside and balance sheet protection; execution and sales accountability.
  • Iger: IP ownership and longevity; audience and emotional resonance; cross-channel monetization.
  • Culp: lean process and waste elimination; ERP and data visibility in Odoo; commercial readiness before scaling demand.
  • Jassy: working backward from a verified customer pain; self-serve and checkout speed; cost-to-serve compression as volume scales.

A proposal does not need nine perfect scores. It needs to survive nine different definitions of good.

05 · The structure underneath

Clean structure makes the vote honest

The board only works because the company underneath it is clean: a parent holding layer holding shared software infrastructure, OpenClaw architecture, CRM snapshots, brand IP, and capital allocation; and operating verticals with distinct P&Ls, B2B SaaS and AI infrastructure, financial services and deal flow, industrial and advisory, and consumer media, IP, and services. Alexander routes a decision to the right vertical first. If the routing is wrong, the 1.5x weight lands on the wrong seats, and the whole model lies to itself.

06 · Why this follows the thesis

Decision first, record second, both verifiable

My first thesis said: running AI like a company, not a chatbot, means identity, approval, action, and result, chained so the record can be trusted. The board is the decision half of that same chain. The audit log is the record half. One without the other is incomplete. A perfect record of a bad decision is still a bad decision. A good decision with no record is unprovable.

The Rubik's image fits because a cube is not nine separate stickers. It is one object, and every turn moves more than one face. Cook voting on a SaaS price change is not off-topic, it is the supply chain square turning on a pricing face. Buffett rejecting a shiny object is not negativity, it is the capital square holding the face still while the others turn.

Lightweight is a design choice, not a compromise. Nine JSON payloads, three passes, one ledger entry. No meeting, no deck, no committee. If a decision model needs a meeting to run, it will not run. Mine runs on every major call, because it is cheaper than deciding alone and more expensive than deciding wrong.

That is the standard I am building toward: a verifiable chain of custody for agent actions, and a verifiable chain of reasoning before those actions happen. Identity, approval, action, result. And before approval, nine seats, three passes, and a human chairman when it ties.
