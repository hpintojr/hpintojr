01 · The finding

Most people are not behind on models. They are behind on management.

The number that stopped me was not a benchmark. It was 18%. Only 18% of organisations keep a complete inventory of the AI agents working for them, according to IBM Think 2026 research cited in the August 24, 2026 AI Tech Radar. That means most organisations cannot answer a basic corporate question: who, or what, is acting on our behalf right now, with what access, and where is the record?

I recognised the contrast because I had spent months building the other way. Not a single assistant with a long prompt, but an ecosystem: a Chief of Staff who decides, a Chief Operating Officer who runs the queue, an HR and Skills Trainer who builds roles, a Compliance Officer, a Security Officer, editors, coders, and a personal assistant for email and calendar. Each one scoped. Each one accountable.

18%

Keep a complete agent inventory. The other 82% do not have a full list of their own agents. IBM Think 2026, via AI Tech Radar, Aug 24, 2026.

88%

Had or suspected an AI agent security or data privacy incident in the past 12 months, in a Gravitee survey of 750 CIOs and CTOs cited in the research. See Sources.

$47k

One recursive agent loop, 11 days undetected, in January 2026. Two agents asking each other for clarification, with no exit logic and no spend cap. Cycles incident record, 2026.

The research conclusion was direct, and I am quoting its shape honestly: on identity and audit, a setup like ours is ahead of most solo setups. On the things that stop a fleet from hurting itself, we still have work to do. Both halves matter. The first half is why I am writing this for this class. The second half is why I am not finished.

“The gaps that matter are not more agents or more roles. They are the systems that keep agents from hurting themselves.”

02 · The lab

What we actually built

We stopped treating agents as prompts and started treating them as hires. A role gets a written brief, a defined scope, a set of skills, and a place in a chain of command. Work does not wander in through chat. It arrives as a written brief, moves through a dispatcher, and lands on an ops board where its state is visible.

That structure mirrors what the field is converging on. Sharbel’s nine-person agency setup uses one manager agent whose only job is to break a request into pieces, delegate, follow up, and return one answer, with specialists running in parallel. Self-hosted fleets like MFS Corp and SMF Works use the same shape: founder, orchestrator, chief of staff, then department heads, each in its own workspace. The pattern is not exotic. What is rare is doing it with corporate discipline at solo scale.

03 · The craft

Context is the work

Anyone can open a chat and get an answer. Running an operation is different. The model needs to know who it is in the org, what it owns, what it must never do, who it reports to, and what good looks like for that role. That is context, and building it properly is a skill most people skip.

The industry is formalising exactly this. Anthropic’s Agent Skills standard treats a skill as a folder: a SKILL.md file with a name and description, plus optional scripts, references, and assets. Only the name and description load at startup, about 100 tokens per skill. The body loads when it triggers, and linked files load on demand. Anthropic calls this progressive disclosure, and its guidance is blunt: start with evaluation, build incrementally from observed gaps, and watch the trajectories, because the name and description decide when a skill fires.

Our per-role training files are the same raw material. Where we differ from the standard, the research named it plainly: without progressive disclosure, every file competes for context at once, and without bundled scripts, deterministic work like sorting, parsing, and form filling gets left to token generation. That is fixable, and it is on the list.

The lesson for this class

Talking to these models well is not a trick. It is management writing: a clear role, a clear scope, clear handoffs, and clear examples. The people who take the time to build skills and workflows properly are not prompting better. They are leading better, in a new medium.

Source: anthropic.com, Equipping agents for the real world with Agent Skills (read in full, Nov 20, 2026).

04 · The discipline

Security is not a setting. It is a structure.

Most solo setups described in 2026 write-ups share keys, keep no inventory, and have no audit trail at all. We built the opposite, on purpose, because these agents touch real businesses: client CRMs, inboxes, calendars, and phone systems that text real customers.

The 2026 consensus, from WorkOS and others, goes further than we do today: one identity per agent per environment, a named human owner, a declared purpose, an end-of-life date, and short-lived credentials, minutes to at most an hour, issued just in time per task, with policy checked at every tool call, not just at login. On May 1, 2026, CISA, the NSA, and Five Eyes partners published the first coordinated advisory on agentic AI and recommended the same direction: phased deployment, progressively increasing access, and replacing static long-lived secrets with ephemeral credentials.

05 · The honest part

Where we are still exposed

A portfolio piece that only lists strengths is marketing. The research found real gaps, and a leader names them. These are the ones I am carrying into the next build cycle.

  • Serial dispatch. Our bridge runs one job at a time on a timer. Everyone else runs specialists in parallel. Our dispatcher is the throughput ceiling for every business at once.
  • Cost controls are the open question. The research could not verify whether per-run caps exist in our setup, and it assumed the weaker case. The failure mode is documented: a $47,000 loop over 11 days, and anecdotal surprises up to $847,000 a month, both with no cumulative spend enforcement.
  • No evals yet. Skills are written but never scored on trajectory or outcome, so drift is invisible. One documented triage agent passed 94% of outcome checks and only 71% on tool-call accuracy. The outcome metric lied.
  • Safety rules that live only in prompts get eaten. Context compaction is lossy by design. In February 2026, an OpenClaw inbox cleanup lost the “confirm before acting” instruction to compaction, bulk-deleted mail, and acknowledged STOP messages without stopping. The fix others use: rules written to files the agent re-reads, fresh sessions per task.
  • No deterministic layer yet. Money-moving and externally visible work, email sends, CRM writes, orders, and SMS, still routes through a non-deterministic agent loop instead of a fixed workflow with an approval gate.
  • Token lifetime is unverified. Whether our per-agent tokens are long-lived or already rotated, whether the audit log is append-only and stored outside agent write access, and whether any cost caps exist are open questions in the research. I am listing them as open, not claiming them as solved.
Compliance is not paperwork theatre

Every AI-generated SMS is A2P traffic. Unregistered traffic is now silently dropped by the carriers, and TCPA exposure runs $500 per unsolicited text, trebled to $1,500 for wilful violations. Each client needs its own brand and campaign registration. An agent cannot automate that away. It can assemble the paperwork, pre-check it, and track consent, and that bundled compliance is part of the offer, not an afterthought.

Sources: Cycles and Rills incident records (read in full, Nov 20, 2026); A2P and TCPA detail from GHL Weekly, Thoughtly, and Contractor Tech Academy, search-index summaries. See Sources.

06 · My thesis

The missing piece nobody is talking about

Here is where I step past the research and give you my own position, labelled as mine. Accountability needs a record nobody can quietly rewrite. That is why our audit log is hash-chained: each entry depends on the one before it, so changing history breaks the chain in a way anyone can detect.

That idea has a name outside AI, and it is blockchain. Not speculation, not coins. The boring, useful part: an append-only, verifiable chain of who did what, when, under whose authority. AI operations and verifiable records need to start going hand in hand. When an agent sends an email, writes to a CRM, or books an appointment for a client, the question a serious business will ask is not “did the model mean well?” It is “show me the record, and prove it has not been altered.”

My thesis, for discussion in AI Exchange

The next standard for agent fleets will not be a better prompt. It will be a verifiable chain of custody for agent actions: identity, approval, action, and result, chained so the record itself can be trusted. We are already building toward that with a hash-chained audit log. The industry conversation needs to catch up.

I do not claim the research proves this. The research proves the audit and identity layer is rare, only 18% keep even an inventory, and that the direction of travel is toward per-action authorisation and legible logs. The blockchain conclusion is my inference from building in both worlds, and it is the discussion I want to start in this class.

07 · The standard

What leaders do differently

Distilled from the research, ranked in the order I would act on them. This is the checklist I am working from, shared openly, because hoarding it helps nobody.

  1. Per-run cost budgets, action quotas, and a kill switch, first. Caps per agent, per turn, and per run. A maximum number of emails, tickets, or deletions per run. One command to freeze the fleet.
  2. Compaction-proof the safety rules. Keep the short list of gated and destructive actions in a file re-read every run, not carried in long context. Fresh session per job.
  3. A deterministic workflow for the money paths. Tools like n8n for email sends, CRM writes, order processing, and SMS. Agents draft and classify. Workflows execute and gate.
  4. Parallelise the dispatcher. A kanban board with dependencies and auto-dispatch. Keep serial only where jobs genuinely conflict.
  5. Evals for every skill. Twenty to fifty golden tasks per role, trajectory scoring, run on every skill or model change. Start with the highest blast radius: the email assistant and the coders.
  6. Short-lived credentials through a gateway. Per-task tokens, separate read and write identities, and an agent that never sees the raw credential.
  7. Confidence-scored approval. Proven, high-confidence actions auto-run. Novel and low-confidence actions pause. That is how an approval queue shrinks from about 40 a week to about 4, instead of becoming a rubber stamp.
  8. Sell the compliance with the agent. A2P and 10DLC registration plus a consent log on every lead record, bundled into the phone agent and CRM offer.

Why I am sharing this in the open

I am not writing this to claim we have finished. We have not. I am writing it because the people in this cohort are the ones who will set the norms for how small businesses run AI, and norms get set by the people willing to show their work, including the gaps. If someone here builds it better than us, that is a win for everyone who has to trust these systems with a real business.

The point of all of it, the org charts, the tokens, the audit chains, is time. Built properly today, these systems give owners time back for their families and their people tomorrow. That foundation starts now, with the unglamorous work: clear context, real skills, honest workflows, and security with structure behind it.
